Diaspora · Heritage

Some connections cross centuries.

Bunce Island is not only a place in Sierra Leone’s past. It has also become part of a continuing relationship between Sierra Leone and Gullah Geechee communities in the United States.

Heritage explainer · Source-based introduction
Core sources

Sierra Leone Heritage documents Bunce Island and the homecoming visits. The U.S. National Park Service provides context on Gullah Geechee history and living culture.

Bunce Island sits in the Sierra Leone River, upriver from Freetown. Its ruins preserve the history of a British slave-trading fortress through which captive Africans were forced into the transatlantic slave trade. The site is painful because it records departure. Its modern history also includes return.

Why the connection matters

Enslaved Africans taken from the wider West African rice-growing region were transported to the coastal Lowcountry of what became the United States. The U.S. National Park Service describes the Gullah Geechee as descendants of West and Central Africans enslaved on rice, indigo and Sea Island cotton plantations along the lower Atlantic coast, where communities retained and developed distinctive language, foodways, crafts, spiritual traditions and other cultural practices.

Sierra Leone occupies a particular place within that larger history. Sierra Leone’s UNESCO tentative-list submission for Bunce Island describes the site as evidence of cultural interchange between Sierra Leone and the United States and specifically points to Gullah communities in South Carolina and Georgia with Sierra Leonean roots.

Homecoming is part of the record

Sierra Leone Heritage records a Gullah “Homecoming” visit to Sierra Leone and Bunce Island in 1989, followed by additional homecoming visits in 1997 and 2005. These journeys matter because they turn historical research into lived encounter: descendants returning to a place connected to forced departure.

1948Bunce Island became Sierra Leone’s first officially protected historic site, according to Sierra Leone Heritage.

Diaspora is also memory

When Sierra Leone To The Top talks about the diaspora, it is easy to focus on remittances, investment, professional networks and return migration. Those are important. But diaspora connection can also mean recovering history, protecting cultural memory and creating space for descendants whose relationship with Sierra Leone began through forced displacement rather than voluntary migration.

That changes the question from “What can the diaspora give Sierra Leone?” to something more reciprocal: “What histories, identities and relationships can Sierra Leone and its global descendants understand together?”

Where Krio and Gullah enter the conversation

The relationship is broader than one monument. Scholars have also examined linguistic and cultural connections across the Atlantic. Smithsonian collections preserve work by linguist Lorenzo Dow Turner, who studied both Gullah and Krio and helped demonstrate the depth of African influence in Gullah language and culture.
